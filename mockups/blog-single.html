Why "best practice" from one culture is a dealbreaker in another

[Placeholder article — a real example from Barbara's client work would replace this.] A rollout that worked cleanly in one office can stall completely in another, and the usual read is that the second team is resisting change. Often they're not. They're applying a different, equally valid logic about how decisions, hierarchy, or consensus are supposed to work, and the rollout was never translated into it.

The pattern

Across mergers, outsourcing transitions and global rollouts, the same thing shows up: a practice gets exported as if it were neutral, when it was actually built around one culture's assumptions about authority, time, or directness. The team on the receiving end isn't being difficult. They're telling you, in the only language available to them, that the assumptions don't hold.

The cost of missing this is not a training problem. It's the fault line that eventually shows up as attrition, or as a transformation that "worked on paper" but never actually landed.

What to do instead

Before exporting a practice, ask what problem it was actually solving in its home context, not what it's called. Ask the receiving team what they already do that solves the same problem, before assuming they do nothing. And treat the friction between the two as information, not resistance to be managed away.

This is what I mean when I say complexity is the raw material, not the obstacle. The differences you're finding uncomfortable are usually the fastest way to see what your plan is missing.

Barbara Mendler

Executive coach, Alluvial
